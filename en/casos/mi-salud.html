Mi Salud

A personal, portable medical record that patients build and share with any professional through a temporary code or a QR.

Role

UX/UI designer · solo project

Type

Personal project · hypothetical solution

Platforms

Mobile app (patient) and web (professional)

Year

2026 · Peru

(01) Context and problem

The starting point

A person's medical history is often scattered across paper documents, memories and different health systems. Recovering and sharing it when needed —when preparing for an appointment, changing providers or in an emergency— can be hard.

Design question

How could patients bring their medical history to any appointment and share it safely, only for as long as needed?

(02) Research

Three experiences, one question

Origin

01

Getting to a specialist

To get an appointment at EsSalud I first had to go through general medicine. I wondered whether having my history at hand would have helped guide my care.

02

My childhood vaccines

I wanted to know which vaccines I got as a child, and I didn't have that information or parents I could rely on to remember it.

03

An emergency

If I have an accident and lose consciousness, how would health staff know my allergies, medications or blood type?

Method

Direct exploration

What we did

We checked what medical information could be retrieved from Minsa Digital and EsSalud: blood type, prescriptions, diagnoses and visits.

Who

Three people: me and two participants, each with their own accounts.

When

October 2026

Secondary research

Legal framework

RENHICE, Peru's National Registry of Electronic Health Records (Law No. 30024, amended by Supreme Decree No. 020-2025-SA).

Technical progress

A Ministry of Health statement on the 2026 Conectatón: clinical data exchange tests in controlled environments.

Preparing for an appointment

A MAPFRE Peru article on what medical history to bring to an appointment.

Findings

01

Expected information doesn't always show up

All three of us found missing records. In my case, two emergency visits (EsSalud and a private clinic) weren't listed, and my vaccines only went up to 2022.

02

Paper is still a source

Both participants kept prescriptions or medication information on paper.

03

Interoperability is advancing, but doesn't explain everything

There's a legal framework and technical exchange tests, but we didn't determine why each record was missing.

04

An appointment calls for many kinds of data

History, surgeries, test results, vaccines, allergies and medications with doses: information that is scattered today.

What the research supports and what remains open

We can claim

I had trouble retrieving my own medical information.

Two participants didn't find all the information they were looking for.

Part of their medical information was kept on paper.

Peru has a legal framework for health record interoperability.

Still open

How many people in Peru face this problem.

Why each record was missing.

Whether people would keep their records up to date on their own.

Whether they would trust a private app with sensitive medical data.

Conclusion

There is a potential need to improve how people gather and share their medical information. Mi Salud is a solution hypothesis, not a validated answer.

That's why the product was designed as a space the patient organizes, not as an official health record: it separates data the user enters from data backed by a document.

(03) Design decisions

Five decisions that shaped the product

01

From appointments to a record

Appointments weren't the core. They became “visits” within a record the patient controls, no matter where they were seen.

02

A temporary code, in addition to the QR

Doctors work mostly on computers, so professionals can also get access by typing a code on the Mi Salud website.

03

Emergency card on the lock screen

Allergies, blood type, conditions and medication visible without unlocking the phone, separate from the full record.

04

Access that expires and can be revoked

Patients choose what to share, see who accessed it and can end access at any time.

05

No promises it can't keep

Each record shows whether the patient entered it or it has a document. The app doesn't interpret results or fake connections with EsSalud, SIS or clinics.

(06) Scope and limits

01

A hypothetical solution

It's a design exercise: I didn't interview health professionals, and the professional view is based on assumptions.

02

Data accuracy

Guaranteeing that medical data is accurate would require government involvement, which is outside this project's scope.

03

Built to grow

The structure of visits, documents and access makes it possible to add new record types without redesigning the app.

(07) Learnings

Researching the health system taught me something as a user: most of us don't know the laws that protect us or how to request our own information.

I started with an app to book appointments from anywhere. Through research, I understood that booking wasn't the underlying problem: the capacity of health facilities is, and an app can't solve that. What is worth making normal is for everyone to have their health information at hand and be able to share it, because gathering it today is complicated.

I also saw that connecting information across insurers and facilities, public and private, is still a technological and institutional challenge. While that process moves forward, it's the patient who carries the burden of finding, gathering and explaining their own data.
